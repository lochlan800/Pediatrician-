Today's review

Learning something once isn't enough — most of it is gone within a week. This brings every lesson back at growing gaps: tomorrow, then three days, then a week, then a month, right at the point you'd otherwise start forgetting it.

Seven things that actually make it stick
1 · Test yourself, don't reread Pulling an answer out of your head is what builds the memory. Reading it again feels productive and mostly isn't. That's what the test at the end of each lesson is for.
2 · Space it out Same total time, spread over weeks, beats one long session — by a lot. That's exactly what this review queue does for you.
3 · Answer before you look The struggle to remember is the useful part. Always have a go before you press "show the answer", even when you're sure you don't know.
4 · Mix it up Your reviews jump between ears, throats, ethics and germs on purpose. Mixed practice is harder and remembered better than doing one topic in a block.
5 · Use the hook Every lesson has a memory hook — an image or a mnemonic. Make your own too, and make them vivid or daft. Odd things stick.
6 · Teach it to someone Explain today's lesson out loud to someone who doesn't know it. The gaps show up instantly, and you can't fake your way past them.
7 · Sleep on it Memories are filed away during deep sleep. A late night undoes a lot of the work you just did.

    What the job is actually like

    The best parts

    • You fix things. A child who couldn't hear properly walks out hearing — sometimes the same day.
    • You operate and you run clinics, so it's never only one kind of day.
    • The widest age range in surgery: newborns to ninety-year-olds, often in one morning.
    • Grommets, tonsils and adenoids mean you work with children constantly.
    • The anatomy is beautiful, and you never stop learning it.

    The hard parts (worth knowing early)

    • Training is long — roughly 15 years from starting medical school — and exams keep coming.
    • ENT is a small, competitive specialty. Getting a training place takes real planning.
    • Not everything is fixable. Permanent hearing loss can't be undone, and you have to say so.
    • Airway emergencies are frightening, and you're the person who's called.
    • Operating millimetres from the facial nerve never stops being serious.

    A typical operating day

    • 07:45 Arrive, see every child on the list and their parents, answer questions, consent.
    • 08:30 Team brief and the safety checklist, said out loud with everyone in the room.
    • 09:00 Operating starts. Youngest first — they cope worst with not eating.
    • 12:30 Between cases: notes, letters, a word with each family in recovery.
    • 14:00 Afternoon list, or clinic — looking in ears, reading audiograms, deciding who needs surgery.
    • 17:00 Ward round, handover, and teaching if you're lucky.
